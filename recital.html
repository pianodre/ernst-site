Senior Recital

Dylan Ernst, piano

Johann Sebastian Bach 1685–1750

Prelude in A-flat Major, BWV 846

Bach wrote this prelude as part of The Well-Tempered Clavier, a collection designed to demonstrate the expressive and technical possibilities of the keyboard in all major and minor keys.

Rather than telling a story, the piece serves as a study in harmony, motion, and balance. Its steady flow and clear structure reflect Bach’s belief that musical beauty comes from order, precision, and thoughtful shaping of simple material.

Frédéric Chopin 1810–1849

Étude in G-flat Major, Op. 25 No. 9 “Butterfly”

Chopin published this étude in 1837 as part of his second set of studies, works that transformed the technical exercise into genuine concert music. The shortest of all his études, it lasts barely a minute.

The study lies in its flickering right-hand figure — quick, light staccato leaps that flutter continuously from the first bar to the last, the motion that earned the piece its nickname. Beneath the charm is a demand for absolute evenness and a feather-light touch at speed.

Ludwig van Beethoven 1770–1827

Piano Sonata No. 8 in C minor, Op. 13 “Pathétique”

  • I. Grave – Allegro di molto e con brio
  • II. Adagio cantabile
  • III. Rondo: Allegro

Beethoven titled this work Pathétique, meaning intense emotion or deep feeling, making it one of the few sonatas he personally named. He composed it at age 28, during the early stages of his hearing loss, marking a significant emotional and stylistic turning point.

The dramatic contrasts, forceful opening, and expressive slow movement reflect both personal struggle and Beethoven’s expanding vision for the piano sonata.

Intermission

Sergei Rachmaninoff 1873–1943

Prelude in G minor, Op. 23 No. 5

Rachmaninoff wrote this prelude in 1901 as part of his Op. 23 set, during a period of renewed confidence following the success of his Piano Concerto No. 2.

The opening march suggests strength and ceremonial power, while the middle section turns inward with a lyrical, reflective character. Together, these contrasting moods highlight the emotional duality central to Rachmaninoff’s style.

Sergei Rachmaninoff

Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini, Op. 43 arr. Michael Loveridge

  • Variation XVIII: Andante cantabile

Rachmaninoff composed this work in 1934 during a low and creatively difficult period following his departure from Russia. At the time, he largely avoided writing completely original large-scale works and instead focused on variation-based compositions.

Using Paganini’s theme, he transforms the material with sharp contrast and brilliance, most famously in the eighteenth variation, where the theme becomes a broad and lyrical melody.

Frédéric Chopin 1810–1849

Polonaise in A-flat Major, Op. 53 “Heroic”

Chopin wrote this polonaise as a powerful expression of Polish national pride. During his lifetime, Poland had been partitioned and was under foreign rule, particularly by Russia, and Polish identity was under constant threat.

The bold rhythms and heroic character of the piece reflect resistance, strength, and patriotism, turning a traditional Polish dance into a symbol of national spirit, power, and defiance.

The Performer

Dylan Ernst

I have been at the piano for seventeen years. I study piano performance at Chapman University with Dr. Janice Park, where tonight’s program is presented as my senior recital.

Since 2019 I have led my own studio across Orange County, teaching students of all ages and closing every teaching year with a May recital. The music gathered here, from Bach to Rachmaninoff and Chopin, is the repertoire that shaped me as a player.

This recital marks the culmination of years of study, performance, and personal growth, and I am grateful to share it with you tonight.

Special thanks to my teachers, Eiki Worcester and Dr. Janice Park of Chapman University, for the years of guidance that led to this evening.
