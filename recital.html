Senior Recital

Dylan Ernst, piano

Johann Sebastian Bach 1685–1750

Prelude in A-flat Major, BWV 862

Bach collected this prelude into The Well-Tempered Clavier in 1722, twenty-four pairs of preludes and fugues written to prove that a single keyboard tuning could serve every major and minor key. This is the seventeenth pair, and tonight the prelude is played on its own.

Rather than telling a story, the piece serves as a study in harmony, motion, and balance. Its steady flow and clear structure reflect Bach’s belief that musical beauty comes from order, precision, and thoughtful shaping of simple material.

Frédéric Chopin 1810–1849

Étude in G-flat Major, Op. 25 No. 9 “Butterfly”

Chopin published this étude in 1837 as part of his second set of studies, works that transformed the technical exercise into genuine concert music. The shortest of all his études, it lasts barely a minute.

The study lies in its flickering right-hand figure: quick, light staccato leaps that flutter continuously from the first bar to the last, the motion that earned the piece its nickname. Beneath the charm is a demand for absolute evenness and a feather-light touch at speed.

Ludwig van Beethoven 1770–1827

Piano Sonata No. 8 in C minor, Op. 13 “Pathétique”

  • I. Grave – Allegro di molto e con brio
  • II. Adagio cantabile
  • III. Rondo: Allegro

Beethoven was 28 when he wrote this sonata, at the same time his hearing began to slowly fail him. He titled this one himself, Pathétique, a word for intense and deep feeling, and the music carries the anger and raw emotion he felt as that loss set in.

A slow, weighted Grave opens the work and then returns twice inside the first movement, interrupting it like a memory that will not stay buried. The Adagio cantabile that follows is among the most recognized melodies he ever wrote, and the closing rondo drives the same restless C minor energy to the end.

Intermission

Sergei Rachmaninoff 1873–1943

Prelude in G minor, Op. 23 No. 5

Rachmaninoff wrote this prelude in 1901, during a surge of confidence that followed the success of his Second Piano Concerto and the end of a long creative silence caused by the failure of his First Symphony.

It opens as a hard, driving march, turns inward for a long lyrical middle section, then returns to the full weight of that Russian march.

Sergei Rachmaninoff

Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini, Op. 43 arr. Michael Loveridge

  • Variation XVIII: Andante cantabile

Rachmaninoff wrote the Rhapsody in the summer of 1934 at his villa in Switzerland, taking Paganini’s last solo caprice as a theme and creating twenty-four variations from it. Originally scored for piano and orchestra, this is an arrangement for solo piano.

In the eighteenth variation, Rachmaninoff turns Paganini’s theme upside down. Every interval that rose in the original now falls, and a sharp, restless theme becomes a broad singing melody in the right hand.

Frédéric Chopin 1810–1849

Polonaise in A-flat Major, Op. 53 “Heroic”

Chopin wrote this polonaise in 1842, twelve years after leaving Warsaw for the last time. Poland had been partitioned out of existence, and the polonaise, once a courtly processional dance, had become a symbol of Polish identity under foreign rule.

This Polonaise’s bold rhythms and heroic character turn that traditional dance into a statement of resistance and national spirit. The middle section is built on a long passage of descending left-hand octaves, the moment the piece is best known for. The powerful, defiant nature of the whole work is what eventually led the public to call it the “Heroic” polonaise.

The Performer

Dylan Ernst

After seventeen years at the piano, Dylan Ernst is completing his senior recital at Chapman University, where he is majoring in Computer Science and minoring in Piano Performance under the instruction of Dr. Janice Park.

Since 2019, he has led his own piano studio across Orange County, teaching students of all ages and concluding each year with a student recital in May. The music gathered here, from Bach and Beethoven to Rachmaninoff and Chopin, represents the repertoire that has shaped him as both a pianist and a teacher.

This recital marks the culmination of years of study, performance, and personal growth, and he is grateful to share it with you tonight.

"Special thanks to my family, whose support made all of it possible, and to my teachers, Eiki Worcester and Dr. Janice Park of Chapman University, for the years of guidance that led to this evening."
